# Bihar's aspirational districts need pipelines, not just pitches

Bihar's 13 NITI Aayog Aspirational Districts — Araria, Aurangabad, Banka, Begusarai, Katihar, Jamui, Khagaria, Muzaffarpur, Nawada, Purnia, Sheikhpura, Sitamarhi and West Champaran — sit at the intersection of two fast-moving but **disconnected** state and national efforts: a newly resourced Bihar sports push (Department of Sports budget up from ₹30 crore in 2022 to ₹680 crore in 2024) and NITI Aayog's Aspirational Districts Programme (ADP), whose "Basic Infrastructure" pillar tracks electricity, roads, toilets and water — not sports or playgrounds ([NITI Aayog Assessment Report](https://www.niti.gov.in/sites/default/files/2023-03/An-Assessment-Of-Aspirational-Districts-Programme.pdf)). Every one of the 13 districts already has a nominal government sports foothold — an operational Khelo India Centre and, in six to eight of them, meaningful counts of newly built panchayat-level grounds — but nearly every documented facility story is one of multi-year delay, budget cuts, or asset loss to theft during stalled construction. Talent is visibly emerging anyway: a Jamui para-athlete became a world champion, Begusarai's Barauni belt supplies roughly 70% of Bihar's football squad, and district-level taekwondo, boxing and rugby pipelines are stirring in Katihar, Aurangabad and Purnia — but almost entirely through individual grit and self-funded local coaching rather than institutional pathways. Meanwhile, corporate CSR — which Schedule VII of the Companies Act explicitly permits for "rural sports" and "training to promote... Olympic sports" — is essentially absent from these districts' sports sector: Bihar receives under 1% of national CSR spend against ~10% of India's population, and no confirmed, named CSR sports programme was found operating specifically inside any of the 13 districts. This is simultaneously the central finding and the central opportunity of this report: these districts are a genuine CSR white space with government co-investment already flowing into basic grounds, meaning a well-designed programme can add coaching, equipment, scouting and competition structure on top of existing public infrastructure rather than having to build everything from zero — but it must do so without the benefit of any sports-specific baseline data, M&E framework, or established local NGO delivery partner, all of which this research confirms are missing or unverifiable at district level.

## Two policy tracks run in parallel with no formal link between them

Bihar's Sports Policy-2026, cabinet-approved on 1 August 2026, and NITI Aayog's Aspirational Districts Programme are, on current evidence, **institutionally separate tracks with no confirmed convergence mechanism**. Coverage of the policy describes talent identification through hunts at multiple levels including the district level, with selected athletes to receive training, infrastructure and financial aid — but the policy text reviewed does not specify a district-level infrastructure mandate or name any districts ([Drishti IAS](https://www.drishtiias.com/state-pcs-current-affairs/bihar-cabinet-approves-bihar-sports-policy-2026)). On the other side, the Aspirational Districts Programme's "Basic Infrastructure" pillar is built around indicators covering electricity, internet connectivity, roads, latrines/toilets, drinking water access, Common Service Centres, and pucca houses — not sports or recreational infrastructure, and the ADP selection/ranking framework references deprivation indicators such as un-electrified households, households without individual toilets, unconnected PMGSY villages, and rural households lacking water access — again with no sports/playground metric ([NITI Aayog Assessment Report](https://www.niti.gov.in/sites/default/files/2023-03/An-Assessment-Of-Aspirational-Districts-Programme.pdf)). Because sports/youth infrastructure sits under Bihar's own Department of Sports rather than the departments (power, PHED, rural works) that feed the ADP dashboard, any sports gains in these 13 districts simply do not register on the "Champions of Change" scoring NITI Aayog uses to track them — sports development here is a state-driven track riding alongside, not through, the aspirational-district framework.

That said, the districts are not sports-policy orphans. Every one of the 13 districts has a Khelo India Centre: per the official Khelo India dashboard's August 2026 list, each of the 13 districts has an operational, "New"-category Khelo India Centre: Araria (Badminton, Collectorate Campus, 30 athletes, PCA hired); Aurangabad (Boxing, Indoor Stadium Aurangabad, 28 athletes, PCA hired); Banka (Kabaddi, Village Gorgama/Amarpur block, PCA hired, athlete count not reported); Begusarai (Kabaddi, Mahatma Gandhi High School, 28 athletes, PCA hired); Katihar (Wrestling, District Khel Bhawan, 30 athletes, PCA hired); Jamui (Football, Shri Krishna Singh Stadium, 30 athletes, PCA hired); Khagaria (Hockey, Koshi College Hockey Ground, 30 athletes, no PCA hired); Muzaffarpur (Wushu, District Khel Bhawan, 24 athletes, PCA hired); Nawada (Rugby, Rugby Club Mirjapur, 25 athletes, PCA hired); Purnia (Rugby, Zila School Purnea, 40 athletes, PCA hired); Sheikhpura (Taekwondo, District Khel Bhawan, 2 athletes, PCA hired); Sitamarhi (Weightlifting, Ward 16 Rampur Parori, 30 athletes, PCA hired); West Champaran (Football, High School Amain/Parasbigha, 32 athletes, PCA hired) ([Khelo India Dashboard](https://dashboard.kheloindia.gov.in/public/front/assets/List_of_KICs_08_2026.pdf)). But utilisation and staffing are visibly uneven — Khagaria's hockey centre has no hired coach, and Sheikhpura's taekwondo centre serves just two athletes against 24–40 at comparable centres — indicating formal coverage without functional depth. None of the 13 districts hosts Bihar's sole Khelo India State Centre of Excellence, which is the Bihar Sports Academy, Rajgir, covering Wrestling, Weightlifting, and Athletics, listed as "Operational" and sits in Nalanda district, outside the target set — meaning athletes from all 13 districts who progress past grassroots level must travel to a hub they have no district-level presence in. One genuine convergence win is confirmed: Bihar hosted the 7th Khelo India Youth Games (May 4–15, 2025) — the first time the Games came to the state — across five venues: Patna, Rajgir (Nalanda), Gaya, Bhagalpur, and Begusarai, with roughly 10,000 athletes, coaches and staff from 28 states and 8 UTs competing in 27 disciplines ([TheWeek/PTI](https://www.theweek.in/wire-updates/national/2025/05/02/cal21-bh-nitish-sports.html)), making Begusarai the clearest documented case of a marquee national event routed through one of the 13 districts.

Statewide, the money is real and growing, but it is not tagged to aspirational-district status. The Department of Sports...has a 2025–26 budget allocation of ₹568 crore, of which about ₹373 crore is earmarked for stadiums/sports infrastructure and about ₹195 crore for sports development and activities ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Sports,_Government_of_Bihar)), while a separately reported figure put the Bihar State Sports Authority's overall budget rose from ₹30 crore (2022) to ₹680 crore (2024), with training spend rising from ₹3 crore to ₹7.5 crore over the same period, and a ₹10 crore prize pool funding the "Mashal" talent-hunt programme ([The Print](https://theprint.in/ground-reports/bihar-is-on-a-sports-gold-rush-rs-680-cr-budget-big-ticket-events-hunt-for-olympians/2403402)). These figures (₹568 crore, ₹680 crore, and a third ₹668 crore programme-envelope figure reported elsewhere) come from overlapping but non-identical reporting periods and could not be reconciled into one time series — a reporting-transparency gap in itself. No scheme located in this research — not the block-stadium programme, the Khel Bhawans, or the panchayat-ground drive — explicitly frames itself around NITI Aayog's "aspirational district" designation; all are described in universal, per-block or per-panchayat terms, meaning the 13 districts benefit only as an incidental by-product of state-wide rollout, not through deliberate affirmative targeting.

## Infrastructure exists on paper, decays or stalls in practice

The infrastructure backdrop is a statewide deficit before it is a 13-district one. Only 49.8% of Bihar's 93,165 schools have a playground, meaning roughly half lack one; Bihar and Meghalaya (43.7% coverage) are named as the lowest-performing large states for playground availability in UDISE+ data, far below top performers like Punjab (97.5%), Delhi (96.5%) and Daman & Diu (99.8%) ([Education World](https://www.educationworld.in/over-23-schools-in-india-have-no-playground-report)). No district-disaggregated UDISE+ figure for any of the 13 districts was retrievable, so this near-50% deficit must be read as the floor, not the confirmed figure, for each district individually.

Where named, district-level facility projects follow a strikingly consistent pattern of delay and scope-cutting. In **Muzaffarpur**, the Sikandarpur Multi-Purpose Sports Stadium saw construction resume in April 2025 after an approximately 15-month halt; the original contractor was removed for slow progress when the project was about 53% complete. Seating was scaled back from a planned 5,000 to 2,000...and a planned sun canopy was dropped for budget reasons. During the halt, cables under the high-mast floodlights were stolen, and the squash court, pavilion, and swimming pool were left incomplete, with the original ₹19.36 crore budget cut by nearly ₹6 crore ([Muzcorner](https://www.muzcorner.in/?p=7397)). In **West Champaran**, the planned Bettiah International Sports Complex — a planned over-Rs-60-crore complex to replace the "rundown" Maharaja Stadium, with an 8-lane synthetic track, imported-grass natural field, gym, canteen...and a VIP spectator gallery — had, as of January 2025, Chief Minister approval and a municipal NOC but the Detailed Project Report had not yet been prepared — i.e., construction had not yet begun ([Patna Press](https://patnapress.com/?p=12255)). **Katihar's** Rajendra Stadium is reported under a ₹28 crore reconstruction and **Purnia** is one of four cities (with Patna, Saharsa, Darbhanga) undergoing land acquisition for a divisional-level sports facility, with a separately reported ₹37 crore complex under construction — though full detail on both could not be independently verified beyond headlines ([Prabhat Khabar](https://www.prabhatkhabar.com/state/bihar/katihar/rajendra-stadium-will-be-reconstructed-with-28-crores/amp); [Patna Press](https://patnapress.com/bihars-grassroots-sports-revolution-stadiums-scholarships-and-a-university-in-the-making/)). No named stadium or complex could be confirmed in sources for the remaining nine districts (Araria, Aurangabad, Banka, Begusarai, Jamui, Khagaria, Nawada, Sheikhpura, Sitamarhi) — a confirmed research gap rather than evidence of absence.

Below the flagship-stadium tier, a genuinely useful infrastructure layer is being built: panchayat-level grounds. Under a statewide scheme (target 8,050 gram panchayats; 5,317 projects approved; 5,285 completed as of August 2026), district-level completed-ground counts show marked unevenness even within the 13-district set: district-level completed-ground counts include several of the 13 target districts: Muzaffarpur (263), West Champaran (176), Purnia (173), Nawada (169), Banka (162), Katihar (151); grounds nearing completion were also reported in Jamui (10) and Khagaria (5), with no equivalent figures...found for Araria, Aurangabad, Begusarai, Sheikhpura, or Sitamarhi ([Indian Masterminds](https://indianmasterminds.com/news/bihar-completes-5285-panchayat-sports-grounds-grassroots-sports-development-220922/)). This produces a practical two-tier map for any new programme: Muzaffarpur, West Champaran, Purnia, Nawada, Banka and Katihar already have a meaningful density of built grounds, while Jamui and Khagaria lag sharply, and five districts (Araria, Aurangabad, Begusarai, Sheikhpura, Sitamarhi) have no public ground-count data at all. Separately, the state's panchayat-ground programme carries a documented unit cost that is directly usable for planning: per-ground construction cost of roughly ₹10–17 lakh, against a 2024 programme budget of ₹521.92 crore ([Indian Masterminds](https://indianmasterminds.com/news/mgnrega-sports-grounds-bihar-rural-sports-revolution-171290/)).

The clearest ground-level case study of what "infrastructure gap" actually means day to day comes from a fencing programme in Motihari (East Champaran — administratively adjacent to, not inside, West Champaran, a distinction the sources make clear and this report preserves). There, a district fencing training centre and a SAI Khelo India Centre operate out of Khel Bhawan, Motihari, together training roughly 60 fencers. The centre began in 2019 with no aluminium piste and insufficient kits for its then-nine fencers, who had previously trained on an agricultural field before being allotted the Khel Bhawan space; equipment improved only in 2022...and recently...Coach Appu Kumar stated Bihar still has no high-performance training centre or National Centre of Excellence, and the centre is partly self-funded via a ₹600/month fee per fencer ([Khel Today](https://kheltoday.com/7th-khelo-india-youth-games-2025/from-gandhis-satyagraha-to-breeding-ground-for-talented-fencers-why-bihars-motihari-is-making-news)). The absence of any NCOE in Bihar is a state-level structural ceiling that affects every one of the 13 districts equally: even a district's most successful athletes must eventually leave the state (Motihari fencers train at Guwahati's NCOE) to progress. One significant evidence gap is flood exposure: despite several of the 13 districts (Araria, Purnia, Katihar, Sitamarhi, West Champaran) sitting in Bihar's flood-prone Kosi–Seemanchal belt, no source directly documented flood or waterlogging damage specifically to sports grounds or stadiums in these districts — the only flood-resilience infrastructure story found concerned health sub-centres in Khagaria, not sports facilities. This should be treated as unresearched, not as evidence that flooding does not damage sports infrastructure there.

## Talent surfaces through grit, not pipelines — Barauni and Jamui as bookends

The talent-pipeline evidence tells a coherent story: real, sometimes exceptional, talent is emerging from these districts, but almost always despite weak institutional scaffolding rather than because of it. The strongest individual case is **Jamui's Shailesh Kumar**, a para-athlete from Islamnagar village, Aliganj block, Jamui, [who] comes from a farming family that faced "financial and personal challenges." He won India's first gold at the 12th World Para Athletics Championships 2025 in Delhi (1.91m, setting three successive championship records of 1.85m, 1.88m, 1.91m), after earlier winning silver at the World Championships in Paris and gold at the Asian Games in China, and was rewarded with a government post under Bihar's "Medal Bring, Job Get" scheme ([Patna Press](https://patnapress.com/shailesh-kumar-gold-world-para-athletics/)). Jamui separately carries a living traditional-wrestling (dangal) culture, with the Sports Minister noting at a 2026 tournament that the district has "long been known for sporting talent," an event that drew 188 wrestlers statewide ([Patna Press](https://patnapress.com/bihar-mati-ka-bal-dangal-jamui-2026/)).

The strongest *collective* case is **Barauni, in Begusarai**, a self-sustaining football nursery built entirely by local volunteers rather than state investment. Barauni has produced 12 state championship titles and accounts for nearly 70% of Bihar's football squad...Sports Authority of India scouts visit local fields, and when players are asked who trained them, "the answer...usually leads back to Barauni" ([Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/sports/football/khelo-india-youth-games-2025-how-barauni-became-bihar-powerhouse-begusarai)). Its coaching capacity has historically come from personal sacrifice, not institutions: Sanjeev Kumar Singh, a former Mohun Bagan goalkeeper, gave up an Army career to mentor village youth, calling football "my calling." After the 1990 loss, the village had to hire coaches and "scrape together" resources — direct evidence that even a nationally-recognised talent hub inside an aspirational district has had to self-finance its own coaching infrastructure.

Elsewhere, pipelines are thinner and newer. **Katihar** is producing taekwondo talent (Payal and Arman, both selected for Bihar/national teams), **Aurangabad** is producing boxing qualifiers, and both Aurangabad and **Banka** have only recently opened dedicated para-sports training centres — each opening explicitly described as giving athletes access "for the first time," implying a prior vacuum ([Prabhat Khabar sources, Aurangabad/Banka]). **Purnia** offers the single clearest documented evidence of regional access failure: at a state rugby officiating seminar, Shubham Anand, from Harimudhi village, Banmankhi block, Purnia...was reported as "the only participant from the entire Seemanchal region" at that seminar — a direct marker of how thin regional access to even administrative/technical sports training is outside Patna-area hubs ([Patna Press](https://patnapress.com/purnia-shubham-anand-seemanchal-rugby-match-officiating-training/)). Since Seemanchal encompasses Purnia, Katihar and Araria — three of the 13 districts — this single data point plausibly generalises across a quarter of the target set.

The best quantified school-sports data point, though not generalisable beyond its own district, comes from **Muzaffarpur's** annual Sports Festival: About 4,000 athletes from 102 schools competed across eight disciplines at L.S. College grounds, with over 700 races run; 46 of the 102 schools won at least one medal; government and private schools competed on the same field, with girls mak[ing] up roughly 32–35% of participants...competing in athletics, football, kabaddi, and tug of war; girls' football participation reportedly doubled compared with the previous year. The festival's organizers argued the main obstacle to female participation is a lack of opportunity rather than culture ([The Bridge](https://thebridge.in/others/the-muzaffarpur-sports-festival-why-indias-sporting-future-is-built-in-schools-55895)). Organisers also flagged the structural dropout mechanism directly relevant to programme design: "a lack of consistent opportunities and a lack of records" as a reason promising students can disappear from the sports system. No comparable participation, dropout, or gender data exists for any of the other 12 districts — this is one of the report's sharpest data gaps, since girls' participation economics (safe transport, kit, and consistent competition calendars) are precisely the kind of thing a CSR programme can move quickly on if baseline numbers existed.

Four districts — **Nawada, Sheikhpura, Sitamarhi, and West Champaran** — returned no individually named notable athlete in this research at all, beyond generic infrastructure or sports-day news; this should be read as a research/visibility gap rather than confirmation of thinner talent, especially given how recently (2024–2026) the state's own investment has begun and how much of the documented talent elsewhere succeeded *before* that investment arrived.

## CSR is nearly absent from sports in these 13 districts — and that is the opportunity

This is the report's single most decision-relevant finding for a prospective CSR programme: **no confirmed, named CSR sports programme from a major corporate foundation was found operating specifically inside any of the 13 target districts.** Large corporates that are demonstrably active in these districts — REC, BPCL, Aster DM Healthcare, JM Financial, HDFC Bank — are deploying CSR into health, education, agriculture, and livelihoods, not sports. REC Limited's substantial Muzaffarpur package illustrates this precisely: REC Limited...undertook Rs 24.38 crore of CSR projects in Muzaffarpur...but these cover a hospital waiting hall, a multipurpose hall, an incubation centre, 25 incubators for the district hospital/PHCs, renovation of 50 Anganwadi centres...and a 200-bed rest house...No sports component is mentioned ([India CSR](https://indiacsr.in/muzaffarpur-gets-rs-24-38-cr-csr-fund-from-rec-for-development-projects/)). Similarly, BPCL's afforestation work touches Aurangabad and Nawada, Aster DM Healthcare's mobile medical vans cover six blocks of Katihar, and JM Financial's rural-development project covers four blocks of Jamui — a real corporate CSR footprint exists in these districts, just not in sports.

The structural reason is visible at the state level. Bihar's Chief Secretary stated that Bihar holds about 10% of India's population but receives less than 1% of national CSR expenditure, and this compounds with a national pattern in which aspirational districts (covering ~15% of India's population) receive only about 2% of total CSR funds, reflecting "limited CSR investments" in these areas generally, not just in Bihar ([India CSR](https://indiacsr.in/companies-need-to-align-csr-efforts-with-local-needs-to-maximize-impact-in-bihar-chief-secretary-amrit-lal-meena); [IDR Online](https://idronline.org/tag/policy/)). Sports CSR specifically is a sliver even of that sliver: nationally, sports CSR spending rose from roughly ₹57.62 crore in FY2014-15 to roughly ₹287 crore in FY2021-22 — growing, but still only 1–2% of total CSR spend ([Vinod Kothari & Company](https://vinodkothari.com/2023/09/csr-spending-in-indian-sports-sector/)). The one Bihar-specific, sports-focused private initiative identified in this research — STAIRS Foundation's "One Bihar One Goal," run with ALPHA Sports Academy — is explicitly statewide in ambition, planning youth clubs "in every village," high-performance centres for 15 sports, and a statewide football talent hunt as a first step, but no district list, budget, or quantified targets are disclosed ([The CSR Universe](https://thecsruniverse.com/articles/stairs-foundation-launches-one-bihar-one-goal-initiative-to-nurture-grassroots-sporting-talent)) — so it cannot be counted as confirmed district-level coverage. National sports-for-development NGOs with strong grassroots models elsewhere (Magic Bus, Tata Trusts' hockey/football portfolio, Vedanta's Zinc Football Programme) show no confirmed Bihar activity at all in available sources — this is a gap in documentation, not necessarily in reality, but it means a new programme should not assume it can simply plug into an existing local NGO delivery network; it will likely need to build or import one.

Crucially, the legal pathway for a CSR sports programme here is unambiguous and favourable. Schedule VII, item (vii) of the Companies Act, 2013 reads: "training to promote rural sports, nationally recognized sports, Paralympic sports and Olympics sports," and separately item (x) of the same Schedule VII...lists "rural development projects" as an eligible CSR category, distinct from the sports clause — meaning a combined rural-development-plus-sports CSR programme in Bihar's aspirational districts could in principle draw on two separate eligible heads of Schedule VII ([CA2013.com](https://ca2013.com/?p=1551)). Legal commentary further confirms that eligible activities include infrastructure development (playing fields, grounds, arenas, athlete accommodation), training (athlete identification, coaching, fitness), running sports academies (including accommodation and education for students), and scholarships, while cautioning that spending that only adds comfort/amenities without a direct link to promoting the sport does not count ([Vinod Kothari & Company](https://vinodkothari.com/2023/09/csr-spending-in-indian-sports-sector/)). The replicable template visible across comparable (non-Bihar) corporate sports-CSR programmes — Reliance Foundation's football pathway (23.02 million children reached over a decade), Hindustan Zinc's Zinc Football Programme in Rajasthan (350+ children across 12 community centres plus 40 at a residential academy), Tata Steel's JRD Sports Complex (~2,500 children/year across 19 disciplines) — is consistently a tiered structure: community-level coaching and talent identification feeding a smaller number of higher-investment centres of excellence, layered with competition structures and scholarships for identified talent ([Reliance Foundation](https://www.reliancefoundation.org/what-we-do/sports-for-development); [The CSR Journal](https://thecsrjournal.in/top-csr-projects-for-sports-in-india)). None of these models has yet been deployed in Bihar's aspirational districts, which is precisely the gap a new programme can fill — and because government-built grounds already exist at meaningful density in six of the 13 districts (Muzaffarpur, West Champaran, Purnia, Nawada, Banka, Katihar), a CSR programme there can skip ground construction and go straight to coaching, equipment and competition structure, while Jamui and Khagaria — and the five districts with no ground data at all (Araria, Aurangabad, Begusarai, Sheikhpura, Sitamarhi) — likely need combined infrastructure-plus-programming investment.

## Funding has to be stitched together — DMF is a dead end, MPLADS and Khelo India are not

No single large, dedicated district sports budget line exists in Bihar. A realistic funding stack for these 13 districts draws on three confirmed channels and explicitly excludes a fourth that is often assumed relevant. **District Mineral Foundation (DMF) funds should be ruled out**: nationally, total DMF collections stood at Rs 60,203.96 crore, with only Rs 30,024.39 crore spent; Odisha (Rs 17,428.70 crore), Chhattisgarh (Rs 8,628.65 crore) and Jharkhand (Rs 8,099.41 crore) held the largest shares, while Bihar's DMF collection was only Rs 100.47 crore with just Rs 6.38 crore spent, and no source identified any of Banka, Jamui or Nawada (the mining-adjacent subset of the 13) as notified mining-affected DMF districts ([Down To Earth](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/news/mining/district-mineral-foundation-has-over-rs-11-000-crore-to-invest-in-mining-affected-districts-58939); [PIB](https://pib.gov.in/Pressreleaseshare.aspx?PRID=1813237)).

**MPLADS/MLALADS is a confirmed, workable channel**, if modest and capital-only. Each MP controls ₹5 crore per annum, and the scheme guidelines explicitly permit "Public recreational facilities, sports and parks" as a permissible work category..., and allow MPs to recommend playfield development — including levelling playgrounds in hilly areas and boundary walls for villages/blocks — drawn from the Khelo India shelf of projects, and in urban areas durable sports assets such as multi-purpose sports halls, athletic tracks, football/hockey turf ([MPLADS Guidelines 2023](https://pbeso.punjab.gov.in/esostatic/Static/PDF/MPLADS/Guidelines/MPLADSGuidelines2023.pdf)). The practical ceiling is low — minimum sanction per work is Rs 2.5 lakh; repairs/renovations are capped at Rs 50 lakh per MP per year — and assets must sit on government land with no provision for recurring maintenance, so MPLADS/MLALADS works best as top-up capital for a single asset rather than a programme backbone.

**Khelo India is the dominant national-scheme lever**, but it flows through state nomination and competition, not automatic per-district entitlement. Cost norms from the scheme's compendium are directly usable for budgeting: "Rs 1.50 crore is adopted as the average cost estimate for a normal centre," with small centres up to Rs 0.75 crore and mega centres up to Rs 3 crore, while State Playing Field Associations can receive one-time seed money of up to Rs 50 lakh...plus a recurring grant of Rs 12 lakh per year per state/UT, and "Model playfields" carry an annual allocation of Rs 25 crore (national, not per-district) ([Khelo India Compendium](https://yas.gov.in/sites/default/files/Compendium%20of%20instructions%20on%20Khelo%20India%20National%20Programme%20for%20Development%20of%20Sports%20Scheme.pdf)). For a genuinely low-cost rural ground, the one concrete figure found is Andhra Pradesh's draft sports policy cit[ing] Rs 5 lakh per playfield funded through MGNREGA labour-intensive construction — a template directly transferable to Bihar's own MGNREGA/panchayat convergence machinery, and roughly consistent with Bihar's own documented panchayat-ground cost of ₹10–17 lakh per ground. A sensible layered budget model for a CSR programme, therefore, is: government land plus MGNREGA/panchayat or MPLADS capital for the basic ground (₹5–17 lakh), with CSR funding reserved for what government money structurally cannot cover well — coaching salaries, equipment, talent scouting, competition calendars and scholarships, all explicitly CSR-eligible under Schedule VII (vii) and not easily funded through recurring government maintenance budgets.

Two things a prospective funder should know are decisively unresolved. First, **no India-specific, sourced M&E framework for sports CSR or government rural-sports programmes was found anywhere in this research** — not from CSRBox, NGO Darpan, Sports Authority of India, or the ADP dashboard methodology itself, which tracks delta rankings across health, education, agriculture, financial inclusion and basic infrastructure but was not confirmed to include any sports/recreation indicator. A new programme will need to build its own monitoring framework from scratch (participation counts, coaching-hour logs, talent-progression tracking to state/national selection, infrastructure utilisation rates) rather than adopt an existing standard. Second, **no sourced per-child or per-coaching-programme cost figure exists in Indian sports-CSR reporting** — published material discloses total reach and total budget but essentially never a per-beneficiary unit cost, meaning any programme budget here must be built bottom-up from coach salaries, equipment, ground maintenance and competition costs rather than benchmarked against a disclosed industry rate.

## What a real programme should do, and what it cannot yet know

Three design implications follow directly from the evidence. First, differentiate by district tier rather than treating the 13 as a homogeneous bloc: six districts (Muzaffarpur, West Champaran, Purnia, Nawada, Banka, Katihar) already have 150+ completed panchayat grounds and should receive coaching/equipment/competition investment layered on existing assets; Jamui and Khagaria have single-digit ground counts and need combined infrastructure-plus-programming; and five districts (Araria, Aurangabad, Begusarai, Sheikhpura, Sitamarhi) have no public ground data at all and require a baseline-data exercise before any capital commitment. Second, prioritise coaching continuity and competition calendars over one-off facility construction — the Barauni and Motihari case studies both show that talent persists even through severe equipment poverty when a committed local coach and a regular competitive outlet exist, while the Muzaffarpur festival organisers independently identified "lack of consistent opportunities and records" — not raw equipment scarcity — as the live constraint today. Third, treat girls' participation as a solvable access problem, not a cultural one: Muzaffarpur's ~32–35% female participation rate, attributed by organisers to opportunity rather than resistance, suggests that safe transport, girls' team formation and visible competition slots could move the needle quickly, though this evidence is drawn from a single district and should be validated, not assumed, elsewhere.

The honest caveat that should travel with every recommendation above is that this entire evidence base is thin and English-language-search-dependent. Four districts (Banka, Begusarai, Jamui, Sheikhpura) returned almost no district-specific sports information beyond isolated headlines; Hindi-language district editions of Prabhat Khabar, Dainik Bhaskar and Hindustan were not exhaustively searched and likely hold meaningfully more block- and village-level detail; and the NITI Aayog Aspirational Districts dashboard itself was never directly queried in this research, meaning the single most authoritative potential source for district-wise basic-infrastructure delta scores remains unconsulted. A CSR programme design team's first concrete action, before committing capital, should be a short primary-data phase — direct outreach to the 13 District Sports Officers, a Hindi-language media sweep, and a direct pull from the Khelo India and NITI Aayog dashboards — precisely because the gaps documented here (no sports M&E framework, no per-child cost benchmark, no district-level UDISE+ playground data, no confirmed local NGO partner) are not peripheral details but the exact inputs a funder needs before sizing a multi-year commitment.
