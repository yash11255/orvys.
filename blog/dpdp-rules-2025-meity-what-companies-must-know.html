The DPDP Rules Are Now in Force: What CSR Teams and Their NGO Partners Must Know

MeitY notified the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 in November 2025, starting an 18-month compliance clock. What it means for companies and the NGOs that hold beneficiary data on their behalf.

Orvys Research Team · 14 November 2026 · 5 min read

India's first comprehensive data protection law is now a working compliance regime, not just a statute on paper. The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) notified the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 in mid-November 2025, operationalising the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. Companies, and the NGOs that run programmes on their behalf, now have a clear clock running.

The timeline

The Act itself stood notified from the date the Rules were published, with most compliance provisions phased in over an 18-month transition window. That gives organisations until roughly mid-2027 to bring their systems into line, though the Data Protection Board of India and certain provisions took effect immediately.

What the Rules require

  • Clear notice and consent before personal data is collected, in plain language, with the purpose stated upfront.
  • A resident Data Protection Officer for organisations classified as Significant Data Fiduciaries, based on the volume and sensitivity of data they process.
  • Breach reporting to the Data Protection Board within tight, prescribed timelines.
  • Extra safeguards for children's data, including verifiable parental consent in specified cases.
  • Data minimisation and erasure once the stated purpose of collection is fulfilled.

Why this is a CSR issue, not just an IT one

CSR programmes collect a surprising amount of personal data: Aadhaar-seeded beneficiary accounts for cash transfers, health and nutrition records through tools like the Poshan Tracker, student attendance and assessment data, SHG member details. Much of that data sits not with the funding company but with the implementing NGO or the technology platform it uses, which makes the NGO a data fiduciary in its own right under the Act.

Companies funding programmes that collect beneficiary data should treat DPDP compliance as part of partner due diligence, not an afterthought. Practical first steps: map every form, app and onboarding flow where beneficiary data is captured; confirm which implementing partners would qualify as Significant Data Fiduciaries; and update consent language and data-sharing agreements between the company and its implementing agencies before the transition window closes.
