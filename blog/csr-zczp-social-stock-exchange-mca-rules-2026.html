CSR Can Now Fund Zero Coupon Zero Principal Bonds: MCA's New Rule 4A

MCA's May 2026 amendment lets companies route up to 10% of CSR spend into Zero Coupon Zero Principal instruments on the Social Stock Exchange. What it is, and what it means for NGOs and companies.

Orvys Research Team · 14 November 2026 · 5 min read

The Ministry of Corporate Affairs has opened a new route for CSR spending, and it runs through the stock market. On 27 May 2026, the MCA notified the Companies (Corporate Social Responsibility Policy) Amendment Rules, 2026, via notifications G.S.R. 415(E) and G.S.R. 416(E), letting companies meet part of their CSR obligation by subscribing to Zero Coupon Zero Principal (ZCZP) instruments listed on the Social Stock Exchange (SSE).

What changed

The amendment inserts a new Rule 4A into the CSR Rules, 2014, and adds a thirteenth item to Schedule VII of the Companies Act: "Subscription to zero coupon zero principal instruments on Social Stock Exchange." Together, these two changes give ZCZP instruments formal standing as an eligible CSR expenditure, something that was previously untested under Section 135.

What a ZCZP instrument actually is

A Zero Coupon Zero Principal instrument is not a conventional bond. It is issued by a non-profit organisation registered on a SEBI-recognised Social Stock Exchange, carries no interest, and is never repaid: the entire subscription amount is deployed for the social cause the NPO is raising funds for. Subscribing to one is closer to a tracked, exchange-listed grant than an investment.

The limits companies should know

  • A 10% cap: companies can direct no more than 10% of their total CSR expenditure for the year into ZCZP instruments.
  • Most of Rule 4 still applies: the notification applies Rule 4 of the CSR Rules to ZCZP implementation, except sub-rules (5) and (6), so ordinary CSR governance, Board approval included, is not waived.
  • Impact assessment is exempted: the notification excludes ZCZP-funded projects from the mandatory impact assessment that otherwise applies to larger CSR projects, since the NPO's own SSE disclosures serve a similar monitoring function.

Why it matters for NGOs and companies

For credible non-profits already listed, or eligible to list, on the Social Stock Exchange, this is a new and more visible funding channel at a time when most implementing agencies remain chronically underfunded (see our guide to CSR-1 registration for NGOs). For companies, it is one more structured option alongside direct implementation and grants to implementing agencies, worth evaluating against existing programme priorities rather than treated as a replacement for them.

The rule is new enough that several provisions, including specific disclosure requirements for ZCZP subscriptions, are still being worked out in practice. Companies evaluating this route should read the official gazette notification directly and consult their CSR committee before committing funds.
