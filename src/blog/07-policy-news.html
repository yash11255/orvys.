The Ministry of Corporate Affairs has opened a new route for CSR spending, and it runs through the stock market. On 27 May 2026, the MCA notified the Companies (Corporate Social Responsibility Policy) Amendment Rules, 2026, via notifications G.S.R. 415(E) and G.S.R. 416(E), letting companies meet part of their CSR obligation by subscribing to Zero Coupon Zero Principal (ZCZP) instruments listed on the Social Stock Exchange (SSE).

What changed

The amendment inserts a new Rule 4A into the CSR Rules, 2014, and adds a thirteenth item to Schedule VII of the Companies Act: "Subscription to zero coupon zero principal instruments on Social Stock Exchange." Together, these two changes give ZCZP instruments formal standing as an eligible CSR expenditure, something that was previously untested under Section 135.

What a ZCZP instrument actually is

A Zero Coupon Zero Principal instrument is not a conventional bond. It is issued by a non-profit organisation registered on a SEBI-recognised Social Stock Exchange, carries no interest, and is never repaid: the entire subscription amount is deployed for the social cause the NPO is raising funds for. Subscribing to one is closer to a tracked, exchange-listed grant than an investment.

The limits companies should know

  • A 10% cap: companies can direct no more than 10% of their total CSR expenditure for the year into ZCZP instruments.
  • Most of Rule 4 still applies: the notification applies Rule 4 of the CSR Rules to ZCZP implementation, except sub-rules (5) and (6), so ordinary CSR governance, Board approval included, is not waived.
  • Impact assessment is exempted: the notification excludes ZCZP-funded projects from the mandatory impact assessment that otherwise applies to larger CSR projects, since the NPO's own SSE disclosures serve a similar monitoring function.

Why it matters for NGOs and companies

For credible non-profits already listed, or eligible to list, on the Social Stock Exchange, this is a new and more visible funding channel at a time when most implementing agencies remain chronically underfunded (see our guide to CSR-1 registration for NGOs). For companies, it is one more structured option alongside direct implementation and grants to implementing agencies, worth evaluating against existing programme priorities rather than treated as a replacement for them.

The rule is new enough that several provisions, including specific disclosure requirements for ZCZP subscriptions, are still being worked out in practice. Companies evaluating this route should read the official gazette notification directly and consult their CSR committee before committing funds. India's first comprehensive data protection law is now a working compliance regime, not just a statute on paper. The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) notified the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 in mid-November 2025, operationalising the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. Companies, and the NGOs that run programmes on their behalf, now have a clear clock running.

The timeline

The Act itself stood notified from the date the Rules were published, with most compliance provisions phased in over an 18-month transition window. That gives organisations until roughly mid-2027 to bring their systems into line, though the Data Protection Board of India and certain provisions took effect immediately.

What the Rules require

  • Clear notice and consent before personal data is collected, in plain language, with the purpose stated upfront.
  • A resident Data Protection Officer for organisations classified as Significant Data Fiduciaries, based on the volume and sensitivity of data they process.
  • Breach reporting to the Data Protection Board within tight, prescribed timelines.
  • Extra safeguards for children's data, including verifiable parental consent in specified cases.
  • Data minimisation and erasure once the stated purpose of collection is fulfilled.

Why this is a CSR issue, not just an IT one

CSR programmes collect a surprising amount of personal data: Aadhaar-seeded beneficiary accounts for cash transfers, health and nutrition records through tools like the Poshan Tracker, student attendance and assessment data, SHG member details. Much of that data sits not with the funding company but with the implementing NGO or the technology platform it uses, which makes the NGO a data fiduciary in its own right under the Act.

Companies funding programmes that collect beneficiary data should treat DPDP compliance as part of partner due diligence, not an afterthought. Practical first steps: map every form, app and onboarding flow where beneficiary data is captured; confirm which implementing partners would qualify as Significant Data Fiduciaries; and update consent language and data-sharing agreements between the company and its implementing agencies before the transition window closes.

The Pradhan Mantri Awaas Yojana-Gramin (PMAY-G) is now operating at a scale that CSR planners in rural India cannot ignore. On 28 May 2026, Union Minister for Rural Development Shivraj Singh Chouhan released the first instalment of over ₹10,021 crore under PMAY-G for 12 states for the 2026-27 financial year, part of a push toward the scheme's target of 4.95 crore completed rural houses by March 2029.

Where the scheme stands

By the Ministry's own reporting to the Lok Sabha in August 2026, 3.12 crore houses had been completed under PMAY-G, with another 79 lakh sanctioned houses under construction. The scheme, running since 2016, has released a cumulative central share above ₹1.31 lakh crore to states and union territories over its most recent five-year run.

The convergence design is the real story

PMAY-G is built to connect with other flagship schemes rather than stand alone: beneficiaries are meant to also be linked to Swachh Bharat Mission-Gramin for sanitation, Jal Jeevan Mission for piped water, PM Ujjwala Yojana for LPG connections, and MGNREGA for construction-linked employment. That convergence design is exactly the logic Orvys applies when advising companies on working with state governments on CSR convergence: the most effective CSR rupee is the one that completes a government programme's last mile rather than duplicating what it already funds.

Where CSR can add value

  • Last-mile finishing: solar lighting, kitchen gardens or water storage for newly completed PMAY-G homes that fall outside the scheme's own scope.
  • Skilling the construction workforce: mason and plumber training tied to local PMAY-G demand, which also strengthens construction quality.
  • Monitoring and grievance support: especially in districts where sanctioned houses are lagging completion, where an implementing partner's field presence can unblock stuck applications.

India's nutrition mission has become one of the largest digitally tracked welfare programmes in the country, and its scale changes how CSR nutrition spending should be planned. On 9 September 2026, Union Minister for Women and Child Development Annpurna Devi launched the ninth Rashtriya Poshan Maah at Varanasi, with the Ministry reporting that more than ₹21,000 crore has been transferred through direct benefit transfer to over 4.65 crore mothers under the scheme to date.

A tracking system CSR teams can actually use

The Ministry's Poshan Tracker app, built under Mission Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0, had registered over 8.93 crore beneficiaries as of May 2026, and is used to identify stunting, wasting and underweight prevalence in near real time across Anganwadi centres nationally. In a written Lok Sabha reply earlier in 2026, the government confirmed that 2 lakh Anganwadi Centres have been approved for upgrade into better-equipped "Saksham" hubs.

What this means for CSR nutrition programmes

Companies already fund Anganwadi infrastructure, supplementary nutrition and early childhood education under Schedule VII, a sector we cover in detail in CSR for child nutrition in the first 1,000 days. The government's own tracker data is a resource CSR teams can draw on directly: rather than selecting a district on reputation or convenience, companies can ask implementing partners to show Poshan Tracker-reported stunting and wasting rates for the blocks under consideration, and target CSR nutrition spend at the centres with the weakest indicators.

  • Infrastructure gaps: Anganwadi Centres not yet on the Saksham upgrade list are candidates for CSR-funded building, water and toilet improvements.
  • Frontline worker support: smartphones, training and incentives for Anganwadi workers entering data into the Poshan Tracker, since data quality determines whether a block gets flagged for help at all.
  • Complementary feeding: locally sourced supplementary nutrition that fills gaps the government's Supplementary Nutrition Programme does not fully cover.

Before designing a nutrition programme, it is worth checking what Mission Poshan 2.0 already funds in your target geography, so CSR rupees extend the government's reach instead of overlapping it.

Ahead of Chhath Puja, Bihar's Cabinet, chaired by Chief Minister Samrat Choudhary, cleared 32 decisions on 10 November 2026, spanning education, employment, infrastructure, agriculture, technology and welfare. Several of them open direct convergence opportunities for companies running CSR programmes in the state.

The headline decisions

  • Dearness allowance hiked to 63% for state government employees and pensioners, effective from July.
  • Bihar Ki Beti Startup Challenge Scheme approved with ₹126 crore in funding to encourage entrepreneurship and research among female students, running across four financial years from 2026-27 to 2029-30, with digital infrastructure and technology tools for women-led startups and researchers.
  • A new Industrial Training Institute (ITI) approved at Basopatti in Madhubani district.
  • 159 prison calling kiosks cleared across 59 jails in the state.
  • Bihar Canal Maintenance Policy 2026 and revised building bye-laws also received Cabinet approval.

Rural housing funds, separately confirmed

Alongside the Cabinet round, the Centre and state also cleared funding for rural housing: a Central share of ₹450.15 crore and a State share of ₹949.45 crore to support homeless rural families building permanent houses, part of the broader PMAY-G push covered in our piece on PMAY-G's FY27 funding release.

Where CSR can converge

  • The new Basopatti ITI needs equipment, trainers and placement linkages before it can deliver outcomes, exactly the kind of skilling gap CSR funding regularly closes; see our guide on skilling projects that lead to jobs.
  • Bihar Ki Beti Startup Challenge beneficiaries are a natural fit for CSR-funded mentorship, incubation space or seed capital that complements, rather than duplicates, the state's own ₹126 crore commitment.
  • Rural housing allocations mean newly sanctioned PMAY-G beneficiaries in Bihar will need the last-mile support, solar lighting, water access, livelihood linkages, that government housing funds typically do not cover.

This is one Cabinet meeting in a state that has announced welfare and infrastructure decisions at an unusually fast pace this year.
