Bihar has spent the last few months rolling out the biggest change to rural employment policy the state has seen in two decades. The Viksit Bharat – Guarantee for Rozgar aur Ajeevika Mission (Gramin), or VB-G RAM G, formally replaces MGNREGA, and Bihar was the first state in the country to implement it.

The headline change is simple: every eligible rural household is now guaranteed at least 125 days of work in a financial year, up from 100 days under the old scheme. The Act was cleared by both houses of Parliament after a lengthy debate, and the unemployment-allowance safeguard from MGNREGA — compensation if work isn't provided within 15 days of a request — has been carried over.

What's changed on the ground

The scheme formally launched on 1 July 2026 as the Viksit Bharat-Gramin Rozgar Evam Aajeevika Guarantee Adhiniyam, 2025. By early November, Bihar had already provided employment to 10.89 lakh rural families, generating 1.79 crore person-days of work. On 5 November, Chief Minister Samrat Choudhary chaired a review meeting and set a target of 3 lakh projects under the mission, which covers four broad areas: water security, rural infrastructure, livelihoods and disaster management.

The funding model has also changed in a way CSR teams should note. Annual budget allocations are now determined at the start of the financial year based on each state's population, per capita income and level of backwardness — rather than demand-based disbursal through the year. Bihar's own contribution is projected to rise sharply, from roughly ₹551 crore under MGNREGA to about ₹2,319 crore in 2025-26, under a new 60:40 central-state cost split. The Centre has separately released ₹6,715 crore in interim funding to cover the remaining months of the scheme's first year in the state.

Why this matters for CSR

VB-G RAM G's four focus areas — water security, rural infrastructure, livelihoods and disaster management — overlap almost exactly with where CSR budgets in Bihar already go. The difference is that the state now has a standing, funded project pipeline in every one of those areas, with its own execution machinery (JEEViKA, the Jal Jeevan Hariyali mission, and Building Rural Development Society among the agencies involved). For a company funding rural livelihoods or water-security work, the more useful question is no longer "where do we start a project" but "which VB-G RAM G-designated project in our district of interest needs a co-funding or skilling partner."

That kind of convergence — CSR money and state-scheme machinery working on the same project rather than parallel ones — is exactly what we've written about before in the context of working with state governments on CSR convergence and the recent PMAY-G housing push. The skilling angle matters too: a guaranteed-work scheme raises incomes, but it doesn't on its own build the skills that let a household graduate out of unskilled manual labour, which is where the kind of programmes we cover in CSR skilling and livelihood projects can plug in directly.

On 30 October, the Bihar Cabinet, chaired by Chief Minister Samrat Choudhary, approved 43 proposals in a single sitting — a mix of big infrastructure spending and quieter policy decisions that matter more for CSR planning than the headline numbers suggest.

The big-ticket items

The largest approval was a ₹11,096 crore power distribution plan. Alongside it, the Cabinet sanctioned ₹18.21 crore for a feasibility study into an underground road corridor in Patna, and ₹12 crore to organise the Bihar State Games 2026. The Cabinet also granted ex-post facto approval to the Bihar MSME Policy 2026, and separately cleared restrictions on the purchase, sale and transfer of land earmarked for new greenfield satellite townships around the state — part of a wider push to expand urban infrastructure announced alongside the cabinet's other recent decisions.

Why the MSME Policy is the one to watch

The power and road numbers will matter for contractors and utilities; the MSME Policy 2026 is the one with direct CSR relevance. Its stated aim is to turn one crore families in Bihar into entrepreneurial households, generating one crore direct and indirect jobs in the process. That's a state target of a scale that no single government scheme can deliver through subsidy and licensing reform alone — it needs skilling, mentorship, access-to-credit support and market linkages running alongside it, which is precisely the kind of work CSR budgets already fund under Schedule VII's provisions for skill development and livelihood enhancement.

For companies already running programmes in this space, the policy gives a concrete, state-endorsed target to align against rather than a generic "promote entrepreneurship" framing. On 8 October, a Supreme Court bench led by Chief Justice Surya Kant, hearing a suo motu case on the treatment gaps faced by patients with Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA), moved to address a problem that has quietly been a CSR question all along: who pays for treatment that costs more than most families, and most one-off charity drives, can sustain.

What the court is asking for

SMA is a rare, debilitating genetic disorder, and the therapies that treat it — gene-replacement treatments and the drug Spinraza among them — can cost several crores of rupees per patient. The Centre currently caps its own financial assistance for rare-disease patients at ₹50 lakh, a limit the Court has separately agreed to reconsider given how far short it falls of actual treatment costs. On 8 October, the bench observed that access to life-saving therapy cannot depend on "sporadic charity or one-off" support, and asked amicus curiae Aparajita Singh to submit, within four weeks, a comprehensive proposal for a dedicated, permanent corpus — to be run strictly on a non-profit basis and funded through a mix of Corporate Social Responsibility contributions, institutional donors, and the Central government.

Why this is a CSR story, not just a health story

Healthcare has long been one of the most-used categories under Schedule VII, but it has mostly meant hospital infrastructure, diagnostic camps, or one-off treatment sponsorships — the "sporadic" model the Court is explicitly moving away from. A court-sanctioned, pooled corpus for a defined set of rare diseases would be a structurally different kind of vehicle: predictable, audited, and designed to outlast any single company's CSR cycle. It's close in spirit to the pooled-funding models we've described in pooled CSR funding collaboratives, and it sits alongside the more everyday healthcare-access work we've covered in CSR and primary health centres — the kind of care that, done well, catches conditions like SMA early enough for the treatment conversation to matter at all.

Companies with healthcare-focused CSR budgets have a genuine reason to track this one closely: if the amicus proposal is accepted anywhere close to its current framing, it would create one of the first Supreme Court-endorsed national CSR funding vehicles in India, and an early, credible contributor to it would be in unusually good company.
