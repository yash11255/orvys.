What is Chhath Puja?

Chhath, also called Surya Shashthi, is a four-day festival of thanksgiving to the Sun god Surya and to Chhathi Maiya, revered as his sister and as a form of the goddess who protects children and family wellbeing. Unlike most Hindu festivals, Chhath involves no idol and no priest-led ritual in a temple. It is performed directly at the water's edge, by the worshipper, in full public view, which gives it an unusually open and communal character.

The four days, and the 2026 dates

Chhath Puja 2026 runs from Friday 13 to Monday 16 November, with the main evening offering on Sunday 15 November. The four stages are:

  1. Nahay Khay (13 November): A ritual bath, often in the Ganga, followed by a single simple meal cooked without onion or garlic, marking the start of the vrat (vow).
  2. Kharna (14 November): A day-long fast broken only after sunset with kheer and roti offered first to Chhathi Maiya, after which a roughly 36-hour nirjala vrat, a fast taken without even water, begins.
  3. Sandhya Arghya (15 November): Families walk to a river, pond or specially dug ghat at dusk and stand in the water to offer arghya, prayers with water and diyas, to the setting sun.
  4. Usha Arghya (16 November): Before dawn the next morning, the same offering is repeated to the rising sun. Only after this is the long fast broken, with prasad shared among family and neighbours.

Offering gratitude to the sun as it sets, before thanking it again as it rises, is what sets Chhath apart from almost every other sun-worship tradition: it honours an ending as much as a beginning.

Where the festival comes from

Sun worship in this form is described in Vedic-era texts, and Chhath's own folklore reaches back further still. The Mahabharata associates it with Kunti, mother of Karna, the son she is said to have conceived through her devotion to Surya, and with Draupadi, who is remembered as observing the vrat for the wellbeing of the Pandavas. Regional traditions in Mithila and the Bhojpur belt also tie it to local legends of kings and sages who regained health or fortune through the same fast. Whatever the exact origin, the festival has been passed down for generations through song, specifically the Chhath geet sung by women in the days leading up to it, rather than through any single scripture.

Why Chhath belongs to Bihar

Chhath is observed across the Bhojpuri, Maithili and Magahi-speaking regions of Bihar, eastern Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand, the Nepal Terai, and wherever communities from these regions have settled, from Delhi-NCR to Mauritius, Fiji, Trinidad and Suriname. But in Bihar it is the festival: the state's biggest annual homecoming, when migrant workers travel back in large numbers, and when the ghats of the Ganga at Patna, Buxar, Bhagalpur and smaller towns across the state fill with hundreds of thousands of worshippers for the Sandhya and Usha Arghya.

A few things make Chhath distinctive as a social, not just religious, event:

  • It crosses caste and class lines. The vrat is observed by families of every background, standing in the same water, often prepared and shared collectively by neighbours.
  • Women lead it. Vratins, the women observing the fast, are at the centre of the ritual, and the festival's songs and customs are passed down through them.
  • It is a civic event as much as a religious one. District administrations build temporary ghats, light and clean riverbanks, and deploy divers and medical teams for the days of the festival, because so much of the public gathers at the water's edge at the same time.

Thekua, and the samagri of the fast

No Chhath offering is complete without thekua, a dense, slightly sweet wheat-and-jaggery biscuit shaped with a wooden mould and deep-fried, which has become as recognisable a symbol of the festival as the sun itself. Alongside it, the offering basket, the daura, typically carries seasonal fruit, sugarcane, coconut, bananas and rice laddoos, laid out on a soop, a flat bamboo winnowing tray, as part of the prasad presented to the sun.

Why it matters for community-focused CSR

Festivals like Chhath are a reminder of what CSR in Bihar is ultimately in service of: the people, rivers and public spaces that make up everyday life here. The same riverbanks that fill with worshippers each November are the ones that need safe drinking water, flood resilience and clean ghats the rest of the year, which is exactly the kind of work we cover in our pieces on safe drinking water along the Ganga belt and flood resilience programmes. Companies that fund work in Bihar through CSR are, in a very direct sense, investing in the same communities that gather at these ghats every year.
